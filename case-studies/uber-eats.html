Uber Eats Ads: Understanding Small Restaurant Ad Behavior

As Uber Eats expanded its in-app advertising offering, we interviewed small restaurant owners, managers, and marketers to understand why ad adoption varies so widely — then translated those findings into research-backed product and UX recommendations.

Timeline

12 weeks

Method

15–20 stakeholder interviews

Focus

Adoption & UX strategy

Benchmarked

DoorDash, Instacart, Instagram

The Problem

Merchants use Uber Eats Delivery — but they don't use Uber Eats Ads. As Uber Eats expanded its advertising offerings, we saw a growing disparity in adoption between Uber Eats Offers and Uber Eats Ads. This project set out to uncover the motivations, pain points, and behaviors of merchants who do (and don't) use Uber Eats Ads, and to understand how restaurant owners perceive Uber's ad tools compared to competitors.

Research Approach

Who we talked to

15 restaurants with fewer than 40 locations each, mostly from Providence, RI, with additional interviews in Milwaukee, WI and Toronto. Owners ranged from 25–65 years old, spanning American diner-style spots, noodle shops, and bakeries.

How we structured it

A two-phase approach: Phase I interviews (broad outreach to 300+ restaurants) to surface high-level pain points and motivations, followed by Phase II interviews using A/B comparisons of Uber Eats Marketing flows to gather low-level UX insight.

0 reported dissatisfaction with the Uber Eats platform overall
0 were skeptical of Ads ROI after using Uber Eats Ads
0 said the current setup & dashboard are insufficient

Three Core Pain Points

Dedicated restaurant owners want a greater stake in the ad campaign process and need to see clear progress to stay retained.

1. Low Confidence in ROI

Ads felt like a black box — no visibility into placement or performance, and skepticism toward estimated metrics and upsell prompts.

2. Lack of Control

Limited ability to target the right audience or schedule ads around slow hours; owners felt their own social media gave them more control.

3. Mental Load

Setup felt complex for owners who "wear many hats" and want simple, set-and-forget campaigns rather than ad jargon to learn.

"I don't trust the recommendations because I think they're just trying to get me to spend more money on their platform." — Duke's Bakery
"I'm the only one running the restaurant, so I still have to prioritize our in-person customers. Ads became unsustainable since I'm the only one managing them." — Tangled Noodles / Y Noodle & Bar

Recommendations

  • Clarify ROI methodology — explain how sales estimates are calculated, and explore "ghost ads" that show merchants their sales trajectory without the campaign.
  • Flexible pricing models — add Pay-Per-Click, Pay-Per-Conversion, and Pay-Per-Impression options so spend ties directly to outcomes.
  • Simple vs. Advanced campaign modes — a streamlined setup for time-strapped owners, and granular scheduling/targeting for marketing-savvy ones.
  • Expanded targeting — by ad timing, demographics, customer frequency, and dietary preference.
  • Metric-driven scheduling — an hourly heatmap of customer behavior (views, revenue, orders) layered directly over the ad scheduler.
  • A clearer analytics dashboard — consolidated performance insight to support confident, informed decisions.

Prioritization

We scored each recommendation on user impact and engineering feasibility to sequence the roadmap.

Outcome

0 of interviewees had a strongly positive reaction to the redesigned flow
0 found the new, comprehensive metrics set significantly more valuable

Once ROI feels transparent and merchants have real control over targeting and scheduling, adoption follows: owners trust the platform enough to stay past the "waiting game" stage that had previously driven churn back to competitors.
